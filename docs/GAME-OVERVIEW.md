# Seat of Power: a full description of the game

*Prepared for an outside reviewer. This describes the game as it stands on 18 October 2026.*

## In one paragraph
Seat of Power is a single-player political simulation set in Nigeria. You run for governor of a Nigerian state, win (or lose) the election, and then govern for up to two four-year terms. Along the way you deal with:
- budgets, debt and federal allocations;
- commissioners, the House of Assembly and traditional rulers;
- a godfather who funded your campaign;
- courts, crises, the press, and an eight-year gold-mining storyline.

Your choices are written into a permanent record that becomes your Wikipedia article, your report card, your successor's fate and, if you earn it, a run for the presidency. All parties and people are fictional. The states, their local governments, the constitution's rules and the institutions are real.

It runs in the browser, phone-first, with a laptop layout. It is also packaged for Vatar (Lagos Life), a Nigerian virtual-world platform, where two optional purchases are offered.

## Who it is for and what it is trying to do
- **The player:** Nigerians, and people interested in Nigerian politics, who want to feel how governing actually works. Every good thing costs money, time or allies. Money comes with strings, and doing nothing also has a price.
- **The tone:** serious but playable. It is not satire, and it is not a lecture.
- **The design rules we hold ourselves to:**
  - Nothing goes on screen without a purpose.
  - Numbers come from the real game state, never decoration.
  - Every decision has a visible consequence.
  - Every blocked action says what blocks it.
  - The adviser's recommendations and the screens always agree.

## The shape of a game
1. **Title screen.** You create a politician profile (name, portrait, state of origin, party leaning). The profile keeps a Hall of Governors of your past careers.
2. **Setup.** "Quick start" fills in sensible picks. Each step can also be changed by hand:
   - **State:** dozens of real states, each with its own economy, problems and difficulty. Lagos is Medium, Kano Hard, Borno Extreme.
   - **Party:** four fictional parties (New Republic Alliance, Young Democrats Movement, United Citizens Party, People's Front Party), or register your own. Each has a ticket price and a strength.
   - **Flagship promise and slogan:** these follow you all game. The news, the adviser and the Wikipedia article quote them.
   - **Running mate:** one of four kinds (grassroots mobiliser, technocrat, party man, newcomer). Each is good at something and risky in another way, and some carry allegations.
   - **Special Adviser:** chosen from a roster of 16. Each has an outlook (fiscal caution, political negotiation, administrative delivery, and so on), a strength, a blind spot, and a small yearly effect.
3. **The campaign.** About six weeks of moves across the state's three senatorial zones: rallies, endorsements, media, the machine and money.
   - Each week the opponent strikes back, before you answer.
   - The screen is a scoreboard: the week strip, you against the opponent, three zone tiles, money and days as chips, and move cards with cost, target zone and strength.
   - A godfather can fund you. The loans are personal debts with favours attached.
4. **Election night.** Results come in from the polling units. Choices about agents and irregularities affect your integrity record.
5. **Tribunal.** A close result can be petitioned, at the Tribunal, then the Court of Appeal, then the Supreme Court. You can lose office in court.
6. **Government House.** You arrive and see your portrait, title, votes and margin.
7. **Governing: Year 1 to Year 4.** This is the heart of the game (see below).
8. **Re-election** at the end of Year 4. It is a campaign again, judged on your record: broken promises make the opponent's attacks hit harder. Losing can be undone once with the paid "Second Chance" on Vatar.
9. **Second term: Year 5 to Year 8.** Then succession: anoint a successor and fight a three-round race for them. A winner may betray you, and your exposure to the anti-corruption agency (EFCC) depends on it.
10. **Endings and afterwards.**
    - **The endings:** voted out, impeached, election nullified, bankrupt, served one term, or served two terms with a successor.
    - **What you get:** a report card with a title (for example "The Builder", "Mr Autopilot", "Promise Keeper", "The Governor Who Owed Salaries") and a grade, plus your Wikipedia article and a share card.
    - **Road to Aso Rock:** a governor's career can continue into a presidential run. It has a party primary across six zones, a running mate, six campaign weeks, and election night under the constitutional rule: the most votes, plus 25% in 24 of 36 states, or a runoff. It is a paid one-off on Vatar and free elsewhere.

## A year in office
Each year is a chapter. The year has steps, shown as a checklist on the Desk:
1. **Set up your government** (first year): convene the Executive Council (ministries and commissioners) and the Traditional Rulers Council. The House won't take a budget from a governor with no cabinet.
2. **Budget.** Split the money across eight sectors.
   - You can start from stance cards: the Budget Office draft, Balanced, Populist, Reformer, or the Godfather's budget.
   - The budget must add up to 100%. It warns you when a split would leave work unpaid.
   - Revenue comes from two sources: the state's own revenue (IGR) and the monthly federal allocation (FAAC).
3. **House vote.** The House of Assembly votes on the budget. Members' loyalty drifts with your party standing, approval, unpaid salaries and corruption.
4. **Policies.** Choose from three tabs: programmes (paid every year they run), bills, and executive orders. Unconstitutional orders are shown under "Beyond your powers".
5. **Projects:** see "Projects" below.
6. **Events.** Up to about four decisions a year, ranked by urgency, so the year never floods. Whatever doesn't fit is handled by your chief of staff and logged.
7. **End the year.** Work progresses, money is paid, needs are judged, and a year review appears.

**The agenda limit.** You get six major actions a year on Medium (seven on Easy, five on Hard). Major actions are starting a programme or project, signing an order, sponsoring a bill, adopting a revenue reform, creating or restructuring a ministry, and opening an investigation. Meetings, inspections and answering events are free. You can answer what matters, but not everything.

## The main systems

### Needs: what the state actually needs
- **What they are:** real problems in named local governments, for example a collapsed road, deaths at the general hospital, schools without roofs, cholera and dry boreholes, highway kidnappings, lost harvests, no power, or displaced families. They are weighted towards the state's weakest indicators.
- **Answering one:** a matching programme or project marks it "Work under way". Finishing the work earns approval and zone support.
- **Neglect costs:**
  - Every year a need waits, it costs approval and zone support.
  - If it is still ignored past its deadline, people die in the news, the need comes back worse, and it goes under Controversies in your Wikipedia article.
- **Idle years:** a year with no new work is punished as "government on autopilot".

### Projects: from approval to a working facility
Projects follow one pipeline:
1. **Propose:** say what, where and how big, in your own words or by answering a need.
2. **Budget:** see the estimated cost, what is approved, any funding gap, and what the budget line can pay. Phasing over several years is normal. A gap means work stops when the money does.
3. **Find the money (if needed):** apply to lenders (a bank syndicate, a state bond, the AfDB and World Bank, or contractor pre-financing). Each says yes or no with reasons. Applying is free; only an accepted offer creates debt.
4. **Procurement:** choose how the contract is let (open tender, selective tender, or direct award, which is pricier and riskier). Then pick from bidders qualified for that sector. Each bidder shows its reliability, quality, speed and integrity. Some are fronts for your relatives or the godfather.
5. **Construction:** progress each year depends on the money released and the contractor. Sites can stall, be suspended, change contractor, or be abandoned.
6. **Built is not working:** hospitals and schools must be equipped and staffed before they serve anyone, and running them costs money every year.

A seven-stage milestone strip shows where each project is: proposed, budget approved, money found, contract signed, under construction, built, open and working. Approval never means a contract has been awarded, cash paid or construction started.

### Money and debt
- **Revenue and salaries:** the federal allocation varies, and salaries must be paid. When the money is short you choose: borrow, pay salaries and hold contractors, or owe workers. Arrears escalate to an NLC ultimatum, then a general strike.
- **Raising money without borrowing:** nine reforms with real trade-offs, such as a digital revenue service, a payroll audit, a Treasury Single Account, a land registry, a market levy and cutting the security vote.
- **Debt management:** ask Abuja for a write-off, push repayments to the next administration, or issue promissory notes at a discount. Each has odds and a price.
- **Campaign loans:** godfather loans are the candidate's personal debts, separate from the state's.

### People
- **Commissioners:** each has a hidden competence, honesty and loyalty that show through their record over time: contract fraud, missed targets, leaked memos, or delivery.
  - **Managing them:** you can meet them (demand results, audit their books, back them with money) and fire them. The consequences depend on their record.
  - **Cabinet balance:** an unbalanced cabinet across the three zones costs the short-changed zone every year.
- **Ministries:** create new ones from a catalogue of 11, or restructure: merge, split, move a policy area, or set up a two-year task force. Every change moves projects, open files and staff explicitly, and a new body is slow in its first year.
- **Secretariat cases:** an allegation becomes a case that moves through stages: meeting the official, gathering evidence, findings, decision. What actually happened is hidden. Evidence is imperfect, so a decision made on thin evidence can come back to haunt you.
- **The godfather:** a living character who can rise to a federal post, fall out with you, defect to the opposition with House members, or die and leave his network to be inherited.
- **The deputy:** can only be removed by the House (Section 188).
- **Rivals, factions and institutions:** these engines run behind the scenes:
  - five named rivals with ambitions;
  - eight factions;
  - six institutions: the House, the courts, the EFCC, the auditor, the press and the civil service, each with its own independence.

  Their pressure arrives as decisions, each answering nine questions: what is happening, who is asking, what they want, the cost, who benefits, who pays, the risk, the debt created, and what the record will say.

### The state's own crises and politics
- **State crises:** 14 templates keyed to each state's real issues, such as oil spills, illegal refineries, bandit "taxes", school abductions, insurgent attacks, farmer–herder violence, floods, sit-at-home orders, erosion, lead mining and land wars.
- **Local governments:** council elections (free and fair, rigged, or caretaker committees) and LG autonomy after the 2024 Supreme Court judgment.
- **Promises:** three per term, one per senatorial zone, judged at the re-election and at the end.
- **The street:** a trending hashtag and voices of ordinary people (some in Pidgin), built from what you have actually done.
- **Courts:** election petitions, National Industrial Court rulings with an appeal option, and judicial reviews of your orders.
- **Trips and investors:** invitations at home and abroad. You can go in person or send your deputy, a commissioner or your adviser. Investors bring jobs and tax.

### The goldfields: an eight-year storyline
A fictional Canadian company, Northstar Mining, finds gold in Year 1, and tensions escalate from Year 2:
- artisanal miners are displaced;
- illegal pits run by foreign buyers with local protectors spread mercury and lead poisoning;
- forest is cut;
- environmental groups go to court.

Mining licences are federal, so the state can't simply grant or revoke them. Six visible measures move with your choices: harm, illegal pits, community grievance, trust, your enforcement readiness, and your leverage with the company and Abuja. A settlement is not a resolution. Lasting resolution needs every measure in good shape and a benefit-sharing agreement, and it is rare (about half of excellent playthroughs by Year 8, almost never otherwise). Your successor inherits whatever you leave.

### Your record
- **The ledger:** every consequential action goes into an append-only record that can't be rewritten.
- **What it feeds:**
  - **The Wikipedia article:** infobox, year-by-year history, controversies, "Record in numbers", "Succeeded by".
  - **Your evidence in court.**
  - **The adviser's warnings.**
  - **The report card.**
  - **Twelve honours,** such as Master Builder, Clean Hands, Fiscal Hawk and Survivor.
- **Corruption** is tracked as several dimensions, not one meter. A "Civic Lens" explains terms like IGR, FAAC and Section 121 in plain language.

## How it looks and feels
- **The compound:** the home screen is a painted map of the state capital's compound: Government House, the Assembly, the courts, the Secretariat, the party office, the palace, the EFCC, project sites and the market. Buildings glow when they need you. On phones, a list sits under the map.
- **The Desk:** a status strip (approval, treasury, party support, corruption), the year's checklist, the adviser, a "What changed" strip, the street, your promises, and what the state needs.
- **The Special Adviser** sits by your desk with a portrait, one short message, and a button that opens the exact task, plus "Why?" and "Later". They speak in the second person ("Governor, the road is approved. The next step is to review its procurement.").
- **Decisions in three layers:**
  - a headline and one or two sentences;
  - decision tiles with real figures and only the actions the game supports;
  - an optional full briefing.
- **Feedback:**
  - stamps (Approved, Funded, Contract signed) that appear only after the decision is saved;
  - result cards showing what actually changed;
  - news anchors with fixed faces;
  - newspaper front pages;
  - short sounds with a mute switch.
- **Art:** about 125 illustrated images (characters, backgrounds, objects). Deputies and attire follow region.
- **Voice (prototype):** the device's own speech engine reads one scene's lines. It is off by default, and text always comes first.
- **Accessibility:** reduced motion (from the device setting or an in-game switch), large tap targets, and visible focus states.

## Difficulty
Easy, Medium and Hard differ in ways you feel:
- how fast approval drains;
- how much corruption and insecurity cost;
- how often the opposition attacks;
- whether the godfather and Abuja act on their own;
- the bankruptcy and impeachment thresholds;
- election-day swings.

States also carry their own difficulty.

## Monetisation (Vatar edition only)
- **Second Chance (₦500, consumable):** rerun a lost re-election once per game.
- **Road to Aso Rock (₦1,500, one-off):** the presidential run.
- **Scenario pack:** planned, not built.

Everything else is free.

## What is not in the game, on purpose
- Real living politicians or real parties.
- An inauguration/"First 100 Days" sequence, and budget-padding decisions. Both were removed by the designer.
- A fresh start as president: the presidency must be earned through a governorship.

## Known weaknesses and open questions
- **Reading load.** The design has deep systems, and the main risk is that it feels like an endless document rather than a lively game. A polished pattern (adviser at the desk, three-layer decisions, stamps, milestone strip, "What changed") now exists for the project sequence only. It still has to be extended to budgets, events, cases and campaigns.
- **Desktop presentation:** some overlays look small on large screens. The game is tuned for phones first.
- **Balance:**
  - the agenda limit of six actions a year;
  - project unit costs, which are simulation values, not real tender prices;
  - how often the goldfields resolve.

  These are design assumptions, checked by simulation but not by real players at scale.
- **Real names in the content:** one lender option still names real Nigerian banks, and some images contain real organisations' text. These are pending a decision.
- **Geography:** only Edo has its real senatorial districts. Other states group their local governments automatically.
- **Voice:** a Nigerian-language speech provider (YarnGPT) was investigated but not adopted. Its terms could not be verified.

## What we would like your view on
1. **First impressions:** from this description, what is the game's strongest hook, and where would a new player most likely get lost or bored?
2. **Depth versus playability:** which systems would you cut, merge or hide to keep it lively without losing the realism?
3. **Pacing:** is a year as a chapter, with six major actions and up to four events, the right rhythm for a phone game?
4. **Realism:** does anything about how Nigerian state government is portrayed seem inaccurate, unfair or one-sided? Think of the godfather, the courts, the FAAC, LG autonomy, the House and traditional rulers.
5. **Presentation:** what would make each decision feel like a game moment rather than a form?
6. **Monetisation:** are the two paid items fair and well chosen? What else would players willingly pay for?
7. **Retention:** what would bring someone back for a second and third career?
